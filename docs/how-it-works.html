SafetyEye · how it actually works

A phone that shouts before someone gets hurt.

No jargon. Eight pictures. If you can follow this, you can explain the whole thing to anyone.

01

Prop a phone facing the dangerous spot.

That's the whole installation. No cameras on the ceiling, no wiring, no computer in a cupboard. One phone, pointed at the thing that can hurt someone.

02

It notices people.

Twelve times a second it looks at the picture and answers one question: is there a person, and where? It draws a box around them. That's all it knows — not who they are, not their face. Just: someone is there.

03

It remembers where you just were.

A single picture can't tell you anything about movement. So it links each box to the one before it and realises that's the same person, a moment later. Now it knows the direction you're walking, and how fast.

04

It works out where you'll be next.

Knowing your direction and speed, it asks: where will this person be three-quarters of a second from now? Not where they are — where they're about to be. This is the part nobody else does.

05

If that's inside the danger zone, it shouts.

Out loud, in words, through the phone's speaker — while there's still time to stop. Not a beep. A sentence, so the person knows what's wrong without looking at anything.

06

If you walk in anyway, it writes it down.

With the time and a photo of the moment. A warning that worked is logged as prevented. A warning that didn't is logged as a breach. The difference between those two numbers is the whole point.

07

At the end of the shift, it writes the report.

One tap. A proper Word document with the photos inside it, faces blurred, ready to send to the client or the insurer. Nobody types anything.

08

And none of it leaves the phone.

Every step above happens on the handset itself. Switch the internet off and nothing changes — because nothing was using it. No video is uploaded, no subscription, nothing to hack.

A site with no network and no budget can run this today.

That is the entire pitch. Everything above is just how.

If someone asks for the technical names

Same eight steps, in the vocabulary an engineer would use. Say the plain sentence first, then the term — it reads as knowing both.
